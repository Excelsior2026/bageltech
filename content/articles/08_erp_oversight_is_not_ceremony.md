---
title: "ERP Oversight Is Not Ceremony"
date: "2026-05-01"
author: "William Parris"
summary: "Steering committees and vendor reports are not oversight. Real ERP governance asks whether the system will actually work under real conditions — and treats shadow systems as evidence, not resistance."
---

Large enterprise implementations generate a predictable ritual. There are steering committees, executive updates, vendor reports, heat maps, and status colors. Everyone becomes very busy managing the visible machinery of oversight. Yet many organizations still emerge from ERP transitions with broken workflows, exhausted staff, and systems that are technically live but operationally misunderstood.

The problem is not usually the absence of oversight. It is the ceremonial nature of the oversight that exists. The process tracks schedule, budget, and formal requirements while missing the operational intelligence that determines whether the system will actually work under real conditions.

That intelligence often lives outside the official project structure. It sits with long-tenured staff, informal knowledge brokers, shadow spreadsheets, local exception handling, and undocumented handoffs between functions. These are often treated as compliance problems or resistance indicators when they are actually evidence about how the institution survives its own formal processes.

Good oversight treats those signals as governance inputs. It asks which shadow systems are load-bearing, which workarounds are covering unresolved requirements, and which informal networks will break if the transition moves faster than the institution can absorb.

Real ERP governance is not about enforcing the implementation plan. It is about maintaining visibility into whether the plan is still connected to operational reality. That requires mechanisms that reward candor, preserve dissent, and give frontline knowledge a path to decision-makers before go-live.

A steering committee that only hears good news is not oversight. It is an audience.
